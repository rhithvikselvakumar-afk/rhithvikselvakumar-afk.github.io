02 / Combinational logic

Seat Belt Alarm Circuit

The project

I constructed a simulated seat belt alarm using inputs for door position, key presence, and seat belt status. The alarm turns on when the door is open, or when the key is present and the seat belt is unbuckled.

Techniques: AND, OR, and NOT gates; truth tables; Multisim; breadboard assembly.

Design process & learning

This project connected a written set of requirements to a logic circuit. I used an inverter for the seat belt signal, an AND gate for the key-and-belt condition, and an OR gate to combine the alarm conditions.

03 / AOI and NAND logic

Majority Vote Circuit

The project

I designed a voting circuit for a four-person board: President, Vice President, Treasurer, and Secretary. A proposal passes with three votes, or with the President’s vote plus at least one other member’s vote.

Techniques: Truth tables, Boolean expressions, AOI gates, NAND conversion, and simplification.

Design process & learning

I translated the voting rules into a truth table, designed the AOI circuit, and converted it to NAND gates. Removing redundant double inversions simplified the implementation before building the final Multisim design.

Logic expression: VST + P(V + S + T)

P = President, V = Vice President, S = Secretary, T = Treasurer. A value of 1 represents a vote to pass.

04 / Logic simplification and troubleshooting

Personalized Seven-Segment Display

The project

I designed a circuit that displays a personalized date sequence on a single seven-segment display. I mapped the required segment outputs in truth tables and simplified the logic using Karnaugh maps before implementing the circuit in Multisim.

Techniques: Karnaugh maps, seven-segment displays, AOI/NAND/NOR logic, and logic testing.

Design process & learning

When the physical display did not initially work, I used a coordinate map of the breadboard connections to find misplaced wires. A logic tester helped me trace signals and check the wiring systematically.

05 / Asynchronous counters and PLDs

Now Serving Display

The project

I designed a two-digit display that counts from 00 to 80, with a Next input and a reset. I included logic to prevent the count from exceeding 80 and uploaded the circuit to a digital prototyping board.

Techniques: Asynchronous counting, programmable logic devices (PLDs), and upper-limit control.

Design process & learning

Using a PLD reduced the amount of discrete wiring compared with my earlier breadboard designs. This helped me understand how programmable logic can simplify a physical implementation and reduce opportunities for wiring mistakes.

06 / Synchronous counters

60-Second Synchronous Timer

The project

I designed a synchronous counter that cycles from 00 to 59 and resets automatically. The circuit uses an MSI counter for the units digit, three flip-flops for the tens digit, and multiplexed seven-segment displays.

Techniques: Clocked logic, flip-flops, carry signals, reset logic, and Multisim simulation.

Design process & learning

The project documentation describes a carry signal that enables the tens stage to increment on the next clock pulse, with custom logic to reset at the tens digit’s 6 state. This extended my counter work from the Now Serving display to a timed sequence.

Across the course

Skills developed

Translating rules into logic

The seat belt and voting circuits connected written requirements to truth tables and gate-level designs. The display project added Karnaugh maps and equivalent gate implementations.

Building & troubleshooting

Breadboarding taught me to organize connections and check wiring carefully. Mapping the display circuit and tracing signals with a logic tester gave me a more systematic way to find problems.

Working with sequential systems

The counter projects introduced designs where the output depends on a sequence of events. I worked with increment inputs, reset conditions, and clocked logic to control the displayed count.

Simulation & implementation

Multisim supported circuit design and demonstration, while breadboards and a PLD prototyping board provided different ways to implement logic in hardware.
