Engineering Design & Development / Team capstone

Electric Bike
Conversion Kit

Making electric cycling more accessible by adapting an existing bicycle, with research and cost analysis guiding the design.

Team: Reed Moore, Trafim Nosko, and Rhithvik Selvakumar

Photos & videosProject presentation

The challenge

An affordable conversion for an existing bike.

Our team set out to design an electric bike conversion kit that would reduce the cost of accessing electric cycling. We considered component cost, installation, maintenance, weight, and power supply while developing and refining our prototype.

Quick installation and broad compatibility were design goals. The project documents a student prototype, rather than a production-ready kit validated across different bicycles.

My responsibilities

I was responsible for the research, engineering calculations, cost optimization, electrical diagrams, and project presentation. I also helped with wiring and CAD design, connecting the planning and analysis to the physical build.

I took the initiative to contact a local hobbyist with experience in electric conversions. Reaching outside our team gave me practice explaining our project, asking focused technical questions, and communicating professionally.

Research & design choices

Our presentation combines customer feedback with a comparison of existing conversion solutions. We compared direct-drive, pedal-drive, and indirect-drive concepts using a decision matrix that considered price, installation time, maintenance, weight, and power supply.

This helped me understand how research becomes useful when it informs an actual decision: which components to choose, which trade-offs to accept, and which user needs to prioritize.

Prototyping & iteration

We began with cardboard and tape to explore component placement, then built two VEX prototypes. The first used one high-torque motor. After feedback from students in a neighboring aerospace class, the second used two lower-torque, higher-speed motors to explore increased speed.

The project then progressed to the motor, controller, battery, and throttle assembly documented in our presentation. I supported the wiring and CAD work while developing the electrical diagrams and analysis.

Electrical planning & calculations

I worked on the electrical diagrams to communicate how the battery, controller, motor, and throttle fit together. The presentation includes the simplified system diagram, component positioning, and CAD designs.

For our runtime calculation, a 36 V, 13 Ah battery corresponds to 468 Wh of nominal energy. Dividing by a constant 350 W load gives approximately 1.34 hours of ideal runtime.

The presentation’s 15–30 mile range is an estimate. Actual runtime and range depend on usable battery energy, losses, terrain, speed, and rider load. We did not establish these figures through a documented full-range test.

Battery & charging decisions

Our team explored building a custom battery configuration, but we did not have access to the spot-welding tools needed to assemble it. We also researched a battery management system (BMS), but the custom battery approach was moving beyond our budget.

These constraints shaped our eventual battery choice and charging approach. We built a custom charging unit, shown in photo 6 of the gallery. This decision taught me to consider the tools and resources available to the team alongside component specifications and cost.

Cost optimization

I worked on comparing component choices and reducing the estimated bill of materials (BOM). Our presentation reports an initial parts estimate of $275.71 and an optimized estimate of $140.47, a reduction of $135.24, or about 49%.

A major change was replacing the listed $120.99 Flipsky controller with a $21.20 Yuandai controller. This taught me to evaluate specifications against the project’s needs and budget.

These are project-era component estimates from slides 32–34, not current supplier quotes or a verified total manufacturing cost including labor, tooling, and overhead.

Feedback & results

The presentation reports that 90% of surveyed testers enjoyed the ride and 88.9% reported no slippage. Speed feedback was mixed: 60% found it adequate, while 40% found it too slow or too fast.

That feedback gave us a reason to keep refining the riding experience. These are results reported in our classroom project survey, rather than evidence of long-term reliability or performance across a broader population.

Reflection

What I learned

Research & optimization

I learned to compare possible solutions and use calculations to support design choices. Cost optimization required balancing the price of a component with the functions our project actually needed.

Bills of materials & manufacturing

I learned where a bill of materials fits into the process of building a product. Organizing parts, quantities, specifications, suppliers, and costs connects a design to purchasing and assembly. Updating that list as the design changes helps a team understand what it needs and what the build will cost.

Teamwork over a long project

A longer project taught me the importance of staying involved beyond my individual tasks. Research, electrical planning, CAD, and assembly affected one another, so communicating changes and coordinating responsibilities mattered throughout the process.

Documentation & presentation

Preparing electrical diagrams, calculations, cost comparisons, and slides helped me explain our decisions clearly. I learned how documentation lets teammates follow the design and how a presentation connects the problem, process, and results for an audience.

Professionalism & communication

Contacting an experienced hobbyist gave me practice introducing our project respectfully and asking clear questions. I learned to communicate technical ideas to people with different levels of experience and to take their time and input seriously.

Leadership & initiative

I learned that leadership can mean recognizing when the team needs more information and taking the first step to find it. Reaching out for outside expertise and taking responsibility for the research and presentation helped me build that confidence.
