PLTW Engineering Design and Development / August–September 2025

Hair Dryer
Reverse Engineering

Understanding an everyday appliance through performance testing, a systematic teardown, and analysis of its design trade-offs.

Team: Rhithvik Selvakumar, Alan Siby, and Niketh Raghu

Test resultsTeam presentation

The brief

What can an existing product teach us?

In this classroom simulation, our team acted as design engineers for a personal appliance manufacturer. We analyzed a competitor’s hair dryer to understand its performance, internal construction, and material choices, then considered how those findings could inform future designs.

My contribution

As a team member, I helped develop testing procedures, worked on functional modeling and the performance spreadsheet, created comparative graphs, and documented the disassembly. These tasks connected the measurements we collected to an explanation of how the product worked.

Planning the analysis

We considered how the appliance is used and how its components support that process. The presentation includes a functional model of the user workflow, followed by testing and teardown documentation. My project write-up also considers the system’s electrical and airflow inputs and its heat, airflow, and noise outputs.

Performance testing

We used a temperature probe, an anemometer, and a sound-level meter to compare the high and low settings. The presentation describes three trials for each maximum-temperature, airflow, and noise measurement. Temperature maxima were observed over one minute; airflow and noise maxima were observed over 30 seconds.

We also timed the drying of a damp paper towel, recorded temperature during warm-up, and measured the appliance’s weight and cord length.

Structural analysis

Our teardown separated the shell, motor, fan assembly, heating coils, mica boards, switch components, wiring, fasteners, and cord. We annotated a photograph and organized component observations in a disassembly chart, recording their functions, dimensions, materials, and interactions.

Measured performance

High and low settings compared

Mean results reported in our team presentation:

MeasurementLow settingHigh setting
Maximum temperature143.5 °F181.6 °F
Air speed12.1 mph20.1 mph
Noise level84.57 dB94.17 dB
Paper-towel drying time50.89 seconds24.42 seconds

The high setting dried the paper towel about 52% faster in our trials, while producing higher temperature, air speed, and noise. These results made the design trade-off visible: greater drying speed came with greater heat and sound output.

Source: team presentation, slides 3–7. These are classroom test results for the tested appliance and setup. Paper-towel drying times do not represent drying times for human hair. The percentage compares the reported mean drying times.

Reflection

What I learned

Testing before drawing conclusions

Repeated trials and a consistent procedure gave us a stronger basis for comparing settings. Organizing readings in a spreadsheet and graphing them helped me connect observations to conclusions.

Understanding a complete system

Taking the dryer apart revealed how many components support a seemingly simple function. I learned to connect electrical energy, motor-driven airflow, and heat output while considering how the parts fit together.

Recognizing design trade-offs

The results showed why maximum output is only one consideration in product design. Drying speed, temperature, noise, materials, and construction all influence the user’s experience and the choices a manufacturer makes.

Documenting technical work

Annotated photographs, component records, and comparative graphs helped turn our teardown into information that another person could follow. I practiced explaining both what we found and why it mattered.
